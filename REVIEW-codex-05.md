**Verdict: no, as a game default to build on without further fixes.** The specific configuration `c2-mid-g` at 1200 Hz remains a reasonable candidate: its NASA pass reproduces, and the improved landing fixtures are useful. Fix those contracts and test the actual wing-force update loop before treating this as the game's foundation. This is not a recommendation to return to the earlier 480 Hz choice.

## Why the numerical choice needs a narrower explanation

Patch 0002 solves the free rigid body's gyroscopic equation implicitly while holding the applied moment explicit. For spherical unit inertia and a roll-only motion, the gyroscopic term vanishes: the rate step is simply `p_next = p + dt*M`. It is not an implicit solve of aerodynamic damping or spring forces. This explains why its pure-damper behavior resembles Euler rather than the unconditional stability of a fully implicit midpoint solve of the entire force law.

The reported damper sweep is useful, but “about seven times stiffer moments” changes its meaning. The 1800 versus 250 numbers apply to a pure damper evaluated inside JSBSim, at different step rates. For the actual external-force path the quoted ratio is only 600 versus 300. They are coarse sampled thresholds satisfying both decay and peak criteria, not exact stability boundaries.

**Verified analytical counterexample; Supported numerical measurements.** I retained the probe's unit inertia and moment law, used `omega=100 rad/s`, `zeta=0.01`, initial angle **0.001 rad**, zero rate, and ran one second. The exact damped oscillator cannot exceed its initial angle. Measured maximum angles:

| Scheme and rate | Moment inside JSBSim | Moment written from outside every step |
|---|---:|---:|
| midpoint, 1200 Hz | **0.002870 rad** | **0.163168 rad** |
| AB4, 960 Hz | 0.001000 rad | **0.060810 rad** |

Thus midpoint does not generally have better spring stability. These deliberately light-damping/high-frequency cases are **outside frozen G7's `zeta=0.3`, 20 rad/s spring**; they do not retroactively change its pass/fail result. They refute using a damping-only sweep as assurance for arbitrary new strip moments. The wider sweep also shows external spring accuracy deteriorating: at 200 rad/s and zeta 0.3, midpoint's maximum angle error is 20.6% of the start, versus AB4's 17.2%.

**Reasoned.** Midpoint still has credible advantages: free-body energy behavior, one rate scheme, no contact-history restart, and a reproduced NASA pass at an affordable measured cost. I would keep it as the next candidate, but choose between it and AB4 using the measured new force law's modes, damping ratios, accuracy and update cadence. Neither candidate has been qualified for an arbitrary wing-strip implementation.

G7's factor two is a useful provisional test point, not a justified reserve covering additional aircraft, lighter inertia, higher speed, nonlinear stall and future force laws together. Doubling a frequency already means quadrupling the corresponding spring coefficient; invoking a generic “6 dB gain margin” does not establish robustness of this coupled, delayed simulation. Pure-damper accuracy can be waived for a clearly defined visual/game tolerance, but short duration alone does not establish invisibility: damping changes integrated attitude and interacts with slower modes.

**Supported practical boundary.** The force-batch experiment uses the shipped `step(n)` interface, not an altered integrator. G7 updates forces at every substep. A game that calculates wing forces once per frame and calls `step(16)` has a much slower feedback loop, even though the integrator still runs at 1200 Hz. Recompute state-dependent forces each physics step or separately qualify the held-force implementation. Adding containment would halt this runaway; it would not make the underlying feedback loop stable.

## Ground safety and what the landing results establish

**Verified.** The positive-projection singularity is real and the floor removes it in compression, compression speed and force projection. The patch leaves STRUCTURE contacts unchanged. Its disabled branch reproduces upstream on the tested key trajectories. These are substantial improvements over simply capping compression.

**Reasoned.** The other limits are engineering safeguards, not established gear physics. Half the CG-to-wheel body-axis height is not measured strut travel. Five aircraft weights of axial force is not a gear-strength specification, and the projected normal force can be twice that. A geometrically unusual aircraft, sloping surface, severe but recoverable landing, changed loading, or longer-travel gear can encounter these limits without having suffered the modeled failure. Zero counters in 46 fixture/rate combinations establish noninterference there, not universal noninterference.

The G2d surrogate `2*W/k` has dimensions of length but is not travel data. It permits each individual wheel's spring to support twice the whole aircraft, with no load sharing, reserve-energy or nose-gear strength model. Certification sink-speed formulas also have specified attitudes, lift assumptions and test conditions; applying the scalar speed does not certify a simulated nose-first arrival. Keep that case as a numerical abuse test and add a properly flared mains-first c172x limit landing. The current 737 and rotor-running AH-1S examples are much better than session 04's fixtures. Trimming at release height in the model's ground effect is sensible; it is not a trim throughout the descent or a validation against actual airplane handling.

**Verified source limitation.** “The wheel is gone” is temporary. The patch has no broken-strut state: as geometry returns below the threshold the wheel can reappear. It abruptly removes and restores a spring/contact constraint. There is no stored-energy or failure-work accounting for this transition. **Reasoned:** this deserves directed burial/re-emergence and attitude-cycle tests; five tiny perturbations are not a passivity proof. My additional 18 cases found no positive cumulative ground work, so I have **not demonstrated a new energy-producing trajectory**. The logged contact resumptions do not by themselves identify overtravel reactivation.

**Supported.** My extra crashes have zero positive cumulative ground work yet closure residual magnitudes up to **15.06 ft**, and all pass G3d. A large crash residual needs step-refinement before attributing it to energy creation; a magnitude alone gives neither its sign nor its cause. It does show why G3d cannot substantiate a complete energy-accounting claim. Falling underground is also not bounded motion simply because the force is bounded.

Use “wheel-force blow-up suppressed in these cases” for patch 0004. For game crash containment, a new gate should require either a physically acceptable continuing state or an intentional halt with a valid retained rendering state. Include height. Do not edit G3d after these results; add a new named gate.

## Energy accounting and its blind spots

**Verified derivation.** With constant mass/inertia, Earth-relative body-axis CG velocity `v` and Earth-relative body rates `w`, the budget uses `K=m*v.v/2 + w.Jw/2`. The off-diagonal inertia signs match `FGMassBalance`'s property convention. `forces/*-total` excludes gravity, so adding weight work separately is right. Coriolis power vanishes. Centrifugal power is `m*Omega^2*(x*vx+y*vy)` in ECEF, with the sign used by the code. The hardcoded Earth rotation rate restricts this calculator to its stated default planet.

There is a very small omitted rotational rotating-frame term for an anisotropic body: `-w . (Omega_body cross (J*Omega_body))`. It is quadratic in Earth rate and negligible at these fixture tolerances, not a plausible explanation for multi-foot crash closure. Changing mass, inertia, moving internal masses or non-Earth planets would require a broader budget.

To check actual closure arithmetic independently, I constructed the spherical Euler pure-damper sequence `p[n+1]=(1-a)*p[n]`, where `a=lambda*dt`, with no translation. For `N` steps the trapezoidal-power residual must be

`R_N = -a*(1-a)*p0^2 * (1-(1-a)^(2N)) / (2*(2-a)*W)`.

For `lambda=180/s`, `p0=0.5`, unit inertia and `W=32.174049`, predicted/measured residuals are **-0.000560354 ft at 480 Hz**, **-0.000267758 ft at 1200 Hz**, and **-0.0000360783 ft at 9600 Hz**. Agreement is within about 2e-17 ft. A nonzero closure residual can therefore be the expected discrete integration error, despite an exact force law and correctly implemented sums. Comparing it across step rates is essential.

**Reasoned limitations.** Ground work measures energy transferred to the rigid aircraft body, not where every joule in the full helicopter goes. Excluding rotor spin energy is acceptable for that body budget if the reported rotor/propulsive forces and moments are included; it cannot validate complete rotor/engine energy conservation. The rotor model's strong rate dependence is a limitation of the helicopter result, even though it does not identify the rate integrator as its cause.

`max cumulative ground work <= tolerance` permits previously dissipated energy to mask later nonphysical additions. The landing runs also dissipate much more forward-motion energy than their vertical impact energy, making this particularly relevant. Per-contact or contact-episode work with spring-storage accounting would be stronger. G3d additionally leaves closure unjudged, so numerical energy can change without violating its ground-work condition. A time-varying ground needs its own surface-motion/work terms; G8's elevation-wave exercise should not be called a stationary rough-runway passivity test.

## Fixtures, references and gate selection

**Verified source; Supported snapshots.** A second `RunIC` does not call every model's `InitModel`. It resets propagated flight state, winds and auxiliary initial state; initializes input/output models; runs model evaluations with integration suspended; initializes derivative histories; and may initialize engines requested by the IC. The simulation time persists in my snapshots. Filters can update using coefficients cached at load even during those evaluations. F-16 elevator position changes from **-0.038361 to -0.038079 rad** on the repeated call. Consequently “keeps exactly engine/control state and resets everything else” is false. The approximation is usable for preparing a fixture, but verify settled residuals and release continuity, rather than treating it as an exact state partition. It also does not reproduce a natural four-second airborne approach. The AH-1S procedure's subsequent six-second hover reduces the concern for its released descent.

**Supported.** The 9600 Hz reference looks adequate for the c172x skip case I refined: its difference from 19200 Hz is far below G8's tolerances. This supports that reference, not all eight cases by extension. Forty-Hz attitude comparisons and final rates are useful for accumulated visible error; reporting peak impact-rate differences separately is fair. However, endpoints use the final common sampled index, and controller stabilization can hide accumulated integration error. Verify reference convergence per case before claiming it universally.

**Verified.** The runway wave is implemented by setting one terrain-elevation property before each step. All wheel contacts then see the same changed spherical ground surface. A real 25 ft wavelength gives different heights and normals under a 737's separated wheels. Call this a repeated-contact excitation test, not realistic rough-runway coverage.

**Reasoned.** Excluding the exact-answer repeated-contact test solely because its loading is synthetic is not a sound argument. Synthetic tests isolate defects precisely because real aircraft, controllers and contact timing can hide them. The 0.039 rad/s endpoint bias is about 2.2 deg/s, not negligible by definition. It should constrain acceptance of the contact-switch design, with a stated error budget and duration/frequency/phase sweep. It does not disqualify the midpoint choice, which avoids the residual bias.

The gates' disclosed numerical limits mostly look like generous regression tolerances around observed default behavior, not external physical acceptance criteria. I found no evidence of hidden post-result tolerance edits. The critical weaknesses are what is omitted: crash height/closure, weakly damped and coupled spring modes, force update cadence, reference refinement for every case, and geometric or failure-state coverage. The prior review's 1200 Hz midpoint NASA result was already known; selecting that rate is not an independent discovery or a blind test. The trial count and disclosure are broadly honest; commit chronology cannot establish that the complete “not seen” list is exhaustive.
